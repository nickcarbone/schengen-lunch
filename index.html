NL

Kapsalon met kipshoarma

Netherlands

Chicken shawarma, chips, melted Gouda

Weeknight 20 min active 30 min total Serves 2 7 items €10.91 · $12.45 $6.22 per head

The kapsalon is twenty-three years old and already a national dish, which is roughly the speed at which Rotterdam does everything. In 2003 a Cape Verdean hairdresser called Nataniël Gomes asked the shoarma counter at El Aviva, in the Delfshaven district, to put everything he liked into one tray: chips on the bottom, shoarma over them, Gouda grated on top and grilled until it ran, then salad and garlic sauce. The staff named it after his trade. Kapsalon is Dutch for hairdressing salon.

Gomes died in July 2023, aged 47. Hundreds of people stood in an honour guard outside his salon. There are not many dishes whose inventor is known by name, still fewer whose inventor was mourned in the street.

One honest note for the episode. The Dutch default shoarma is pork — Albert Heijn's standard tray is seasoned pork shoulder at €9.90 a kilo, and lamb is the premium line at more than double that. Chicken is common and entirely legitimate, but if you walk into a snackbar in Delfshaven and say nothing, pork is what you will be handed.

Method

  1. Heat the oven to 220 °C. Spread the chips in a single layer in two shallow foil trays and bake until properly crisp, about 20 minutes. Undercooked chips are the one thing that ruins this.
  2. While they bake, fry the shoarma strips in a hot dry pan for 5–6 minutes until the edges catch. Do not crowd the pan.
  3. Pile the meat over the chips. Cover thickly with the grated Gouda.
  4. Back into the oven, or under the grill, for 3–4 minutes — until the cheese has melted and pooled rather than browned.
  5. Off the heat, top with shredded lettuce, chopped tomato and raw onion. Garlic sauce over the lot, sambal in stripes. Eat out of the tray.

Drinks

Heineken or a Rotterdam craft pils
Wine
Not a wine dish
Spirit
Jonge jenever, cold, afterwards
Albert Heijn Vierambachtsstraat, Rotterdam-West

Ten minutes from Delfshaven, in a street of Turkish and Moroccan butchers — the honest backdrop for this one. Buy the shoarma at the supermarket if you want the standard product, or at a counter butcher if you want it good.

Pollo al ajillo

Spain

Chicken in garlic

Weeknight 15 min active 45 min total Serves 4 7 items €10.45 · $11.92 $2.98 per head

There is no origin story for pollo al ajillo, and that is the point of it. Spanish food writing is blunt about this: there are no exact data on where or when it began, only that garlic and chicken have been cheap and available on the peninsula for as long as anyone has been writing recipes down. It is a plato popular, económico y sencillo — a dish of ordinary people, built from four things.

Those four things are chicken, garlic, olive oil and salt. Everything else is regional argument. La Rioja and the Basque Country put in guindilla. The south adds vinegar or a slug of Jerez. Restaurants have since added wine, stock, mushrooms and herbs, and Spanish cooks will tell you at length that each addition is either the authentic version or a betrayal, depending on who raised them.

The technique carries the dish. Whole cloves go in first to perfume the oil; chopped garlic goes in near the end so it stays sharp. Get that order wrong and you have chicken with burnt garlic, which is a different and much worse dish.

Method

  1. Joint the chicken small — ten pieces from a bird, thighs halved. Small pieces are what make this fast. Salt them and leave 10 minutes.
  2. Heat a generous film of olive oil in a wide pan. Add one head of garlic, cloves unpeeled and lightly crushed. Let them colour and perfume the oil, then lift them out.
  3. Brown the chicken hard in that oil, in batches, skin side first. Return it all to the pan with the reserved cloves.
  4. Add the sherry, let it hiss away, cover and cook over low heat 20–25 minutes until the thigh juices run clear.
  5. Finely chop the second head of garlic. Stir it in for the last 2 minutes only, off the boil, so it stays pungent. Parsley, a squeeze of lemon, and bread or fried potatoes to mop the pan.

Drinks

A cold fino or manzanilla — the same sherry that went in the pan
Spirit

PT

Bitoque

Portugal

Steak, egg, chips and rice

Weeknight 20 min active 30 min total Serves 2 8 items €10.33 · $11.79 $5.89 per head

The name is a loan word wearing a disguise. Bitoque is a Portuguese corruption of the English beefsteak, carried into Lisbon's taverns by British and Galician workers between the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and worn smooth by a hundred years of use. The Portuguese Academy of Sciences dictionary now defines it plainly: a steak, fried or grilled, with rice, fried potatoes, a fried egg and sometimes salad.

It is the default lunch of the tasca, the neighbourhood tavern, and it has survived every fashion that has passed through Portuguese eating since. When BSE hit in the 1990s, pork quietly became an accepted alternative protein and the dish carried on regardless.

A survey of Lisbon tascas this July found complete bitoques between €6.50 and €10, in neighbourhoods tourists actually walk through. That is the number to put on screen: the cost of making it at home is barely under the cost of being served it.

Method

  1. Put the rice on first — it is the only thing here that takes time. Rinse, then cook in salted water with a bay leaf.
  2. Cut the potatoes into batons and fry them. This is the step people skip and should not.
  3. Beat the steaks thin. Season, then sear hard in olive oil with a smashed garlic clove, 90 seconds a side for medium. Lift out and rest.
  4. Deglaze the pan with the white wine, scraping. Swirl in the butter off the heat. That is the sauce — there is no more to it.
  5. Fry the eggs in a separate pan, whites set, yolks loose. Plate: steak, sauce over, egg on top (a cavalo, on horseback), chips and rice alongside.

Drinks

Beer
Super Bock or Sagres, in a tall thin glass
Wine
A young Douro red, served cooler than you would expect
Spirit
Aguardente, standing up at the counter
Pingo Doce Rua 1º de Dezembro, Baixa, Lisbon

Central, filmable, and the butcher counter cuts bifes to order. Ask for them thin — the whole dish depends on the steak cooking in two minutes.

Flygande Jacob

Sweden

Flying Jacob

Weeknight 15 min active 40 min total Serves 4 7 items 161 kr · $16.26 $4.07 per head

In the summer of 1976 a man named Ove Jacobsson, who worked in air freight, was asked to bring a main course to a bring-a-dish party. He opened his fridge and combined what was in it: shop-roasted chicken, cream whipped with chilli sauce, bananas, crisp bacon and salted peanuts, baked together and served with rice.

His neighbour Anders Tunberg worked at the magazine Allt om Mat. The recipe ran that same year, in issue 13, pages 44–45, under the name Tunberg coined from Jacobsson's trade and surname. The magazine suggested boiled rice and green salad alongside, and a rosé called Perlerose.

Fifty years on it is on supermarket recipe pages, in freezer cabinets and in the standard repertoire of Swedish home cooking, and it has spawned a family of variants with their own names: Simämande Jacob with fish, Svävande Jacob vegetarian, Flygande Kurre with smoked pork loin. Foreigners find the bananas alarming. Swedes find the question tedious.

Method

  1. Heat the oven to 225 °C. Strip the roast chicken off the bone in large pieces and spread in an oven dish.
  2. Fry the bacon until properly crisp and set it aside. It does not go in yet.
  3. Peel and slice the bananas over the chicken.
  4. Whip the cream to soft peaks, fold in the chilli sauce, season, and pour the lot over.
  5. Bake 20 minutes, until bubbling and lightly coloured.
  6. Out of the oven, scatter the crisp bacon and the peanuts over the top — after baking, so both stay crunchy. That order is in the original recipe and it matters. Rice alongside.

Drinks

Beer
Any Swedish lager; Norrlands Guld has the right lack of pretension
Wine
The original printed pairing was a rosé, and a rosé still works
Spirit
Skip it — this is a Tuesday dish
ICA Maxi Lindhagen, Kungsholmen, Stockholm

Big, bright and filmable, with a rotisserie counter by the entrance — the grillad kyckling is the whole shortcut, so buy it hot.

Kibinai

Lithuania

Karaim meat pastries

Weekend 45 min active 110 min total Serves 5 7 items €8.97 · $10.24 $2.05 per head

At the end of the fourteenth century Grand Duke Vytautas brought around 400 Karaim families to Lithuania from the Crimean peninsula, as spoils of war, and settled them at Trakai beside his lake castle. They were a Turkic-speaking people following a scriptural form of Judaism, and they stayed. Kibinai are their pastry: a crimped crescent, the pleated seam running along the top like a rope.

The community is now very small. Around 240 Karaim remain in Lithuania and only a few dozen in Trakai itself. The pastry, meanwhile, has gone national — sold at roadside stops, bus stations and petrol stations across the country, usually by people with no connection to the Karaim at all.

That gap is the episode. A dish can outlive the community that made it by an order of magnitude, and most of the people eating it will not know whose it was.

Method

  1. Rub the cold butter into the flour with a pinch of salt until it looks like coarse crumbs. Work in the sour cream and one egg yolk to a firm dough. Wrap and chill 30 minutes — this is the rest that makes the pastry short rather than tough.
  2. Meanwhile mix the beef with very finely chopped raw onion, salt, plenty of black pepper and a splash of cold stock. The filling goes in raw. Do not brown it.
  3. Roll the dough thin and cut 10 discs about 14 cm across.
  4. Spoon filling onto one half of each, fold over, and crimp the seam into a rope — press, fold the corner, press again, all along the edge. Untidy crimping leaks.
  5. Brush with beaten egg. Bake at 200 °C for 30–35 minutes until deep gold.
  6. Rest 5 minutes. The filling makes its own broth inside; bite carefully or wear it.

CZ

Kuře na paprice

Czechia

Chicken in paprika cream

Weeknight 20 min active 60 min total Serves 4 8 items 139 Kč · $6.51 $1.63 per head

Czech food writing is unusually honest about where this came from: it is a leftover of Austro-Hungarian cooking, absorbed so completely that people forgot it was ever borrowed. It arrived from the Hungarian lands as paprikáš, and Czech kitchens softened it with cream, which is the local move in almost everything.

It used to be made with a hen that had stopped laying, boiled long enough to give in. That is why the older recipes take two hours and the modern ones take one: the bird changed, not the method.

It sits in an odd, useful place in the repertoire — recipe sites file it simultaneously as an everyday weekday dinner and as a Sunday family lunch. Serve it with dumplings, noodles, gnocchi or rice, and every Czech family has an opinion about which.

Method

  1. Salt the thighs well. Brown them skin down in butter in a heavy pot, then lift out.
  2. Soften the finely chopped onion in the same fat. Now take the pot off the heat before the paprika goes in — paprika burns in seconds over direct heat and turns the whole dish bitter. This is the one unforgivable error.
  3. Stir the paprika through the onions off the heat, return the chicken, add stock to come halfway up, and simmer covered 35–40 minutes until the meat gives.
  4. Lift the chicken out. Whisk butter and flour to a paste, drop it into the liquid and cook 5 minutes to thicken.
  5. Temper the sour cream with a ladle of hot sauce, then stir it in off the boil so it does not split. Sharpen with lemon juice.
  6. Return the chicken. Serve with bread dumplings, egg noodles or rice.

Beer
Pilsner Urquell, unfiltered if you can get it
Wine

HU

Lecsó kolbásszal

Hungary

Pepper and tomato stew with sausage

Weeknight 15 min active 45 min total Serves 4 7 items Ft2 918 · $9.14 $2.29 per head

Lecsó without meat is a side dish. Add sliced kolbász and it becomes lunch — that is not a liberty, it is how Hungarian sources describe the distinction. The country goes further and makes a sausage specifically for the purpose: lecsókolbász, a spiced smoked link produced for no other dish.

The base is yellow pointed peppers, tomato, onion, salt and ground paprika, cooked in lard or bacon fat with no added water — the vegetables give up enough of their own. It is high summer food, made when peppers are cheap and good, and in late August Hungarian kitchens turn out enough to jar for winter.

The usual foreign shorthand is Hungarian ratatouille. It is not wrong, but it undersells it: lecsó is a base as much as a dish, and half the Hungarian repertoire starts here.

Method

  1. Slice the sausage into coins. Render it gently in the lard until the fat runs orange, then lift the coins out.
  2. Add sliced onion to that fat and cook until soft and translucent, not browned.
  3. Off the heat, stir in the paprika. Same rule as Czechia: direct heat burns it.
  4. Back on low heat, add the peppers cut into thick rings. Cover and sweat 10 minutes.
  5. Add the tomatoes in wedges and the sausage. No water. Cook uncovered 15–20 minutes until it is a thick stew in its own juice.
  6. Optional and traditional: beat two eggs and stir them through at the end to thicken. Bread to mop.

Antoine-Augustin Parmentier learned what potatoes were worth while a prisoner in Germany, and spent the rest of his life persuading the French to eat them. In 1773 he won a prize for proposing the potato as a famine crop. He grew them around Paris and served Benjamin Franklin a dinner made of nothing else.

He did not invent the dish either. Hachis parmentier appears under that name much later, on a bistro menu dated 29 June 1892, seventy-nine years after his death. It is named in his honour, the way a street is. In French kitchens parmentier now simply signals potato.

Method

  1. Boil the potatoes whole in their skins in salted water. Peel them hot, which is unpleasant and correct, and mash with butter and hot milk. Season hard.

  3. Add the stock and let it reduce until the meat is moist but not wet. Taste and season. Parsley if you have it.
  4. Spread the meat in a gratin dish, top with the mash, and rake the surface with a fork — the ridges are what brown.
  5. Scatter the cheese over. Bake at 200 °C for 25–30 minutes until the top is crusted and the edges bubble.

Nobody can prove who invented Britain's most famous curry, and the attempt has been made formally. The best-known claim belongs to Ali Ahmed Aslam of the Shish Mahal in Glasgow, who said he improvised it in the 1970s when a customer complained his chicken tikka was too dry — spices, cream and a tin of condensed tomato soup.

In 2009 a Glasgow MP campaigned to have the city officially recognised as the dish's birthplace. The bid failed, because establishments across the UK came forward with claims of their own. Food writers are sceptical of the whole exercise. Leena Trivedi-Grenier compares it to asking who invented chicken noodle soup: adding cream, butter and tomato to tandoori chicken that had dried out is an obvious move that many cooks would have made independently. She is sharper still about the national-dish framing — how do you colonise a country for a century and then claim one of its dishes as your own.

Aslam died in December 2022, which is when the argument last flared. The dish is genuinely the thing Britain cooks and orders; the origin story is the part to be careful with.

Method

  6. Fold the charred chicken through at the end so it keeps its edges. Basmati and coriander.

Albanian qofte are separated from every other Balkan meatball by two things in the mix: crumbled feta, and a heavy hand with dried mint and oregano. Stale bread soaked and squeezed goes in as the binder, the patties are dusted in flour, and they are fried rather than grilled.

The regional split is real. Korçë makes them as flat fried discs. Tirana runs to skewers. In the north the same instinct produces Të Dheu, the earthenware-baked version.

They are served with chips or mashed potato and eaten as a main course, not a starter. The name comes from the same Turkish and Persian root as köfte and kofta — pounded meat — which is the through-line of this entire non-Schengen leg: every country on it inherited the same word and made something different with it.

Method

  1. Soak the stale bread in water, squeeze it out hard, and crumble it into the mince.
  2. Add grated onion, crumbled feta, chopped parsley, dried mint, dried oregano, salt and pepper. Mix thoroughly with your hands.
  3. Rest the mixture 20 minutes in the fridge. It firms up and the herbs bloom.
  4. Shape into patties about 2.5 cm thick and roll each in flour.
  5. Shallow-fry in hot oil, 3–4 minutes a side, until deeply browned and cooked through. Two batches; a crowded pan steams them.
  6. Drain briefly and serve hot with chips or mashed potato, and lemon.

What this book is not

It is not a price survey. Two of fifteen countries have shelf-verified prices because two of fifteen retailer websites answered — Albert Heijn and Tesco. The rest block automated retrieval, so their baskets are modelled: a reference European price for each ingredient, scaled by that country's food price level index, converted at the day's reference rate. The method is stated below so you can argue with it. Treat in-country totals as good to roughly a tenth, not to the cent, and treat the ranking as more reliable than any single number in it.

FX rates
ECB euro foreign exchange reference rates, 23 September 2026; Bank of Albania official rate, 22 September 2026. Euro area: $1.1411 to the euro. The lek is quoted by the Bank of Albania because the ECB publishes no lek reference rate; its implied EUR/ALL cross of 91.05 differs from the Bank of Albania's own 91.40 by 0.4%, a consequence of different fixing times.
Country price levels
Eurostat prc_ppp_ind, category Food, price level index EU27 = 100, reference year 2024. Range in this book: Bulgaria 87.1 to France 111.5.
United Kingdom
Not in the Eurostat EU27 series. Its index of 101.5 is derived here from three Tesco shelf prices measured against the same reference basket — implied values of 96.6, 99.2 and 112.9.
US prices
Anchored on BLS average retail food prices, US city average, August 2025, uplifted to September 2026 using the BLS CPI twelve-month changes to August 2026: food at home +2.2%, meats/poultry/fish/eggs +1.1%. Items outside the BLS basket are modelled at US retail and badged MODEL. These are US city averages; DC-area shelf prices run above them.
Yields
Each dish priced at its own natural yield. Portugal and the Netherlands serve two, Lithuania five, the rest four. Per-head is therefore the comparable figure, not basket total.
Histories
Every origin claim in this volume was checked against a primary or institutional source where one exists. Where a claim is contested — chicken tikka masala, currywurst, saltimbocca — the entry says so instead of picking a winner.

Three things worth knowing before you shoot

  • Two of these dishes have named inventors who died recently. Nataniël Gomes of the kapsalon died in July 2023 and hundreds stood in an honour guard for him; Ali Ahmed Aslam of the Shish Mahal died in December 2022 and the tikka masala argument restarted over his obituary. Both are living memory, and both families are reachable.
  • The paprika rule runs through the middle of Europe. Czechia and Hungary both fail the same way — paprika stirred into direct heat turns bitter in seconds. Teaching it once and calling back to it is worth more than teaching it twice.
  • Three of the fifteen are inherited from an occupier and pointedly changed. Bulgaria answered Ottoman kebab with pork. Lithuania kept a Crimean community's pastry after the community had all but gone. Britain claims a dish whose own historians say the claim is the colonial habit repeating itself. That is the spine of a series, if you want one.
